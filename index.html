01 / About me

I'm Ian Ding, a physics graduate from Heilongjiang University, China, who took a detour into C, C++ and Linux. After nearly four years at a certain software company in China, I'm back in student mode, studying for an MSc in Computer Science at the University of Liverpool, United Kingdom.

My working days involved building and improving software on Linux with colleagues, chasing bugs, and asking why something was slower than it ought to be. GDB and perf were frequent companions. The bugs were less welcome.

I'm curious about C/C++ systems, operating systems, runtimes and memory management, compiler optimisation, and parallel computing and CPU/GPU performance. Networked systems and practical algorithms also tend to send me down a rabbit hole. I like questions I can explore by building something, measuring it, and finding out which assumption was wrong.

Alongside my MSc, I'm looking for Computer Science PhD opportunities starting in 2027 in these areas. More systems to understand, more experiments to run, and presumably a few more bugs to negotiate with. This corner collects the things I learn, make, and haven't quite figured out yet.

03 / A few things I've worked on

AT WORK · LINUX · DATA PROCESSING

Getting data to the right place

In one Linux-based C/C++ data-distribution system, I worked on message validation and assembly, routing, configuration and timed tasks. During a major rewrite with a colleague, I took responsibility for the core processing program.

The less visible part: deploying and testing it on Linux, examining function costs with perf, adjusting processing logic and CPU affinity, and using jemalloc for memory allocation. Asking a program to be faster is easy; finding out where the time goes takes a little more detective work.

AT WORK · LINUX · INTEGRATION

Making different systems agree

Across other Linux-based C/C++ data-processing tools, I extended modules, adapted interfaces and data formats, and supported testing and deployment. Integration often meant checking captured traffic byte by byte, tracking down endianness and format mismatches, and coordinating fixes with other developers. Two programs can disagree over surprisingly small things.

EARLIER · LINUX · TRAINING PROJECTS

Learning by wiring things together

Earlier C/C++ training on Linux included contributions to a file-transfer service and a text-search tool: file operations, resumable transfers, a word-suggestion dictionary, and thread-pool and Reactor frameworks based on established designs. A practical introduction to sockets, concurrency and all the ways pieces can fail to fit.
